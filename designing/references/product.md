# Product

Read this when the design shapes what users get: their jobs, who they are, and trade-offs between scope and experience.

## The user

The user is whoever consumes the work. For an interface, that is the person using it. For a library or an internal API, it is the developer who calls it. The person who maintains the code next is a user as well. Judge each decision from their seat, and say what it does for them.

## Experience first

The product is the experience, and every technical decision helps or hurts it. When what is convenient to build conflicts with what serves the user, choose the user.

- **Every feature, control and option earns its place.** Saying no is most of the work.
- **Fewer, finished features beat more rough ones.**
- **The core loop comes first.** A feature serves the central workflow, or stays out of its way.
- **The details are the experience:** feedback, error states, transitions, alignment.
- **Prototype before committing.** A decision about how something looks or feels is cheaper to make in a throwaway prototype than in production code.

Foundations decide the order of the work. The experience decides what it is for.

## Jobs and people

Before a solution, understand the job the user is trying to get done, and the outcomes they measure it by. Record it as a job to be done: the job statement, the steps of getting it done from start to finish, and the outcomes ranked by importance. Record who the users are as personas: their situation, what they want, and what stops them today.

Write the frame's problem in terms of the job, and its acceptance scenario from a persona's point of view.

## Shaping an idea

An idea starts as a few statements of what it is. Shaping it into a concept says what it is through its parts (properties, as noun phrases) and what it does (features, as verb phrases), the benefits it brings, the questions still open, and what it deliberately leaves out. A concept is ready to design against when its open questions are answered or recorded as assumptions.
